# Judge John Overton and Travellers Rest · exhibit brief

Owner direction and research, November 13, 2026. The history is a small, self-contained feature in the 2D portfolio and a fuller, artifact-led experience inside Travellers Rest. Begin with Judge John Overton’s early law career and follow how he acquired land and wealth, built the plantation, and helped Andrew Jackson win the presidency. Move briefly to his son John, Confederate use of the house, and the Union victory at Nashville. **The last chapter is emancipation in 1865.** Reconstruction and the son’s later career are outside the exhibit.

The current house book covers the first residents, enslaved household, campaign, and law reports. The world already has the judge’s portrait, a map table, a writing table, a correspondence chest, and a native book reader. The new history can build on those affordances. The compact 2D article below is now implemented in the local portfolio; see [the first-edition delivery](SHOWCASES_AND_2D_DELIVERY.md). This brief and the [research image set](../assets/travellers-rest/archive/history-research/README.md) remain ready for fuller exhibit authoring. That scene work and hosted release have not occurred.

## The story, in exhibit order

| Chapter | Supported account | What to put in the visitor’s hands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. A lawyer comes to Nashville · 1787–1795** | Born in Virginia in 1766, Overton began his legal career in present-day Kentucky in 1787. He moved to Nashville in 1789, then part of North Carolina’s Mero District, and was admitted to the bar in 1790. He and Andrew Jackson sometimes represented clients together and became land speculation partners in 1794. George Washington appointed Overton a federal revenue supervisor in 1795. [Tennessee Historical Society biography](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/john-overton/) · [Museum’s early Nashville history](https://www.historictravellersrest.org/history/nashville-from-frontier-to-home-of-the-u-s-president) | The held 1795 Tennessee map at the entry. Label it as a period mapmaker’s view; keep the house book’s [Mississippian site history](https://www.historictravellersrest.org/history/the-first-residents-of-historic-travellers-rest) within reach. An optional [federal distillery tax ledger](https://teva.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/collection/p15138coll35/id/216) shows what revenue administration recorded. |
| **2. Building Travellers Rest · 1796–1799** | Overton bought the initial 320 acres in 1796, the year Tennessee became a state. Work began on the house in 1799 with free and enslaved labor. The museum traces the estate’s eventual growth to about 2,300 acres. Skilled people held in slavery worked as brickmakers, weavers, blacksmiths, and cooks, among other roles. This labor built and sustained the plantation. [Museum’s property history](https://www.historictravellersrest.org/history/nashville-from-frontier-to-home-of-the-u-s-president) · [NSCDA Tennessee preservation record](https://www.nscdatn.org/preservation/) · [A Past Uncovered](https://www.historictravellersrest.org/history/a-past-uncovered) | A small house plate and a date card at the writing table, next to the household records. Use the later HABS survey or Highsmith photograph as a clearly dated view of the restored house. The museum’s early plat and property map are links until image reuse is cleared. |
| **3. Law, land, and wealth · 1804–1820** | In 1804 Overton succeeded Jackson on Tennessee’s Superior Court; he later served on its successor, the Supreme Court of Errors and Appeals, until 1816. He and Thomas Emerson brought out the first two volumes of the *Tennessee Reports* in 1813–1817. Overton built substantial wealth through land speculation and business, held a prominent role in banking, and participated in the slave trade. In 1819 he, Jackson, and James Winchester founded Memphis on land they held together at the Fourth Chickasaw Bluff. Situate that development in the context of the 1818 cession of Chickasaw lands to the United States. Overton married Mary McConnell White May in 1820; their household grew and the house was enlarged. [Biography](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/john-overton/) · [Memphis history](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/memphis/) · [Chickasaw Nation treaty timeline](https://timeline.chickasaw.net/Calendar-Companion?categoryname=Treaties) · [Museum’s account of the house](https://www.historictravellersrest.org/history/nashville-from-frontier-to-home-of-the-u-s-president) | Earl’s portrait and the reports at the desk. The held *Murfree v. Leeper* facsimile records a 1791 decision Overton later reported; the case predates his judgeship. Use a small editorial map locator for Memphis, clearly separate from the 1795 facsimile. |
| **4. A friend runs for president · 1822–1832** | Overton helped organize the Nashville Junto and worked with Felix Grundy to secure the Tennessee legislature’s 1822 nomination of Jackson. In the 1824 contest Jackson led the electoral count without a majority; the House chose John Quincy Adams in 1825. Overton helped defend Jackson against attacks on his marriage to Rachel during the 1828 campaign. Jackson won that election, took office in 1829, and continued to receive Overton’s advice. Credit the campaign network and Overton’s specific role. [Biography](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/john-overton/) · [Library of Congress election guide](https://guides.loc.gov/chronicling-america-1828-presidential-election) · [Jackson Papers timeline](https://www.loc.gov/collections/andrew-jackson-papers/articles-and-essays/andrew-jackson-timeline-1767-1845/pursuing-the-presidency-1822-1837/) | Open the campaign drawer to the held [satin victory ball invitation](https://www.loc.gov/item/2020772132/) and the newly held [opposition handbill](https://www.loc.gov/item/2008661734/). It is campaign propaganda, not a neutral account or the source of the marriage attacks. Put the held June 1829 Jackson letter and November 1832 Overton letter on an optional correspondence tab. The invitation was issued December 10 for a ball planned December 23; it does not establish who attended. |
| **5. The people in the records · 1832–1833** | In 1832 the judge purchased two brothers, William, twelve, and Franklin, seven. The museum reports that the inventory made after the judge died in 1833 listed 53 enslaved people by name and age, some in family groups. That number is a snapshot from one document. Connect the brothers’ names here to the people who built and worked at Travellers Rest earlier in the visit. The sale records alone do not establish the boys’ later lives. [William’s record](https://travellersrestenslavedpersonsdocumentationproject.omeka.net/items/show/100) · [Franklin’s record](https://travellersrestenslavedpersonsdocumentationproject.omeka.net/items/show/101) · [Museum account of the inventory](https://www.historictravellersrest.org/history/a-past-uncovered) | Open the existing William and Franklin records by name from the house book. Give the records the same size and legibility as the political artifacts. Until the actual inventory scan and transcription are confirmed, use a sourced summary with a clear label. |
| **6. A house at war · 1864** | By the Civil War the judge’s son John Overton and his wife Harriet Maxwell Overton were living at Travellers Rest. John supported the Confederacy after initially opposing secession; the Colonial Dames’ account says he offered the house to General John Bell Hood, and the museum confirms Hood used it as headquarters in December 1864. Union forces led by George H. Thomas defeated Hood’s Confederate Army of Tennessee on December 15–16. Black Union soldiers in the United States Colored Troops fought at nearby Peach Orchard Hill, then part of the estate. [Museum’s Civil War exhibit](https://www.historictravellersrest.org/current-exhibitions) · [Colonial Dames’ site account](https://www.greatamericantreasures.org/destinations/historic-travellers-rest/) · [Library of Congress battle account](https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/december-16) · [NPS account of a soldier at Overton Hill](https://www.nps.gov/people/kennedy-charles.htm) | One short paragraph about the son and one inspectable battle map. Use a restrained locator for the house and hill only after verifying positions against the map and museum sources. An optional caption can name Pvt. Charles Kennedy of the 12th United States Colored Infantry, whose unit’s initial assault at Overton Hill failed. Distinguish that action from the overall Union victory. |
| **7. Emancipation · 1865** | The 1863 Emancipation Proclamation did not cover Tennessee. On February 22, 1865, Tennessee’s Unionist voters approved a state constitutional amendment abolishing slavery. The Confederacy’s armies surrendered in 1865; Lee’s surrender in April was followed by others. On December 6 the 13th Amendment was ratified nationwide, abolishing slavery and involuntary servitude with an exception for punishment after conviction of a crime. Abolition changed the law; it did not by itself secure equal rights. End there. [Tennessee Historical Society on the 1865 vote](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/government/) · [Tennessee ratification ticket](https://teva.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/collection/p15138coll18/id/1493) · [NPS on Confederate surrenders](https://home.nps.gov/apco/learn/historyculture/surrender-events-after-appomattox.htm) · [National Archives amendment and transcript](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment) | Give the last open spread to the Tennessee vote and the federal amendment, with the national document and its legible text. An optional short reference to the [July 1865 petition of freedpeople in Lincoln County, Tennessee](https://www.freedmen.umd.edu/Bright.htm) can show what freedom meant to people asking to protect their wages and families. It is a regional source, with no known connection to the plantation. |

Use **Judge John Overton (1766–1833)** for the father and **John Overton, the judge’s son** at the transition. The museum calls the son “Colonel,” an honorary title; use his relationship on first mention so the battle is clearly after the judge’s death. **Travellers Rest** follows the museum and portfolio spelling. Avoid ranking fortunes, assigning a modern dollar value, or claiming that everyone at the plantation gained freedom at a known instant.

## 2D: a brief history feature

Use one compact **History of Travellers Rest** card below selected work or in the “About” area, with the credited Earl portrait if useful. The card can open a short semantic article with the following copy and a few source links, or contain it directly if the layout allows. Make the full ending available in plain text, including to visitors who never enter the world. Keep **Explore in 3D** optional. The portfolio, résumé, and project reports retain the primary navigation.

> Judge John Overton came to Nashville in 1789 to practice law. He became a Tennessee judge, helped publish the state’s first law reports, and amassed wealth through land speculation and other ventures. Enslaved people helped build his plantation home, Travellers Rest, and their labor sustained it. Overton and his allies helped Andrew Jackson win the presidency in 1828.
>
> In 1864 the judge’s son John made Travellers Rest available to Confederate General John Bell Hood, whose army lost the Battle of Nashville. Tennessee voters approved abolition in February 1865. That December, ratification of the 13th Amendment abolished slavery nationwide, except as punishment for a crime after conviction.

Link the museum’s history, its records for William and Franklin, and the National Archives from the plain article. The estate’s existing no-WebGL house book should also expose the short ending and source links.

## 3D: a small exhibition inside the existing library

Use **Read the history** by the portrait and at the writing table. Give visitors a bound history sequence within the house book experience and shortcuts to objects in the room. A numbered contents slip makes the route visible at any point. The entry map sets the place; the portrait and reports introduce the lawyer; the writing table pairs the plantation’s growth with the names of people who built and worked there; the campaign drawer puts satin and newsprint side by side. Close with the map and an open emancipation spread in a quiet spot at the same table. The battle and emancipation together take two short stops.

Let the materials do the work: warm walnut and brass around unaltered scans, restrained warm light on the opening table, and an evenly lit closing spread with generous space for the text. Keep source labels and dates consistent. Use flat, high-contrast text and a zoom view for manuscript reading. Display facsimiles and later documents as present-day exhibit objects in a reconstruction. Avoid period claims about where these things sat in 1864, staged battles, or invented likenesses and voices for enslaved people.

The portrait can introduce objects from the judge’s lifetime, with its dramatization label and captions. Switch to curator text or a clearly separate optional narrator for enslavement records and events after 1833. The interactive portrait may answer a question about later history in curator mode with citations; it should never claim to have witnessed it. Let visitors continue, skip, or return to the same place and page without audio or camera motion.
